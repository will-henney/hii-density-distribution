1.2.1. High density tail will come from proplyds

  1. Generate a single-proplyd PDF
    • based on N(r, θ) laws from Dyson models
    • With scale factors (n0, r0), the peak density and i-front radius
  2. Integrate over known distributions of proplyd parameters
    • Size distribution from Henney & Arthur, or Vicente (although those are overestimates) or Tsamis:2013a or one of the more recent papers like Ballering:2023a or Boyden:2025a
      • Boyden find r0 ≈ (12 AU) 10**{R / 0.05 pc} which is bizarre
      • This means that the solid angle increases with R, which gives an increased influence of the further proplyds
      • But this cannot carry on forever. They only fit it over range 0 to 0.06 pc (30 arcsec), for which their largest radius is 100 AU
        • So at that point, r/R ~ 0.01, so θ ~ 0.01 rad = 0.6 deg
        • Ω / 4 pi = 0.5 (1 - cos(0.6 deg)) = θ2 / 4 = 2.5e-5
        • So even with 200 proplyds, we only get a total of 5e-3, so 0.5% of the total emission measure. And that is probably an overestimate
      • Vicente:2005a is bad for the close-in disks, but should be better for the further out ones. The chord diameter maxes out at about 1 arcsec for R > 100 arcsec (0.2 pc), which corresponds to radius of about 200 AU
    • Peak density distribution from same sources, or just use the theoretical value (note that Boyden densities are wrong)
    • Spatial proplyd distribution can come directly from catalogs like Ricci:2008a
      • Or we can use the fits from Henney & Arthur, although these are only validated over the inner part
    1. Spatial distribution of proplyds
      • Combine two distributions:
        1. Spatial distribution of young stars
        2. Fraction of stars with disks

1.3. Contribution of turbulence to the PDF

  • We could assume it is log-normal
  • But there is a very good discussion in Beattie:2022a as to why it cannot actually be log-normal at all scales
  • They show two alternative models that include intermittency
    • compound log-Poisson model
    • Langevin model
  • Both give an s-pdf with increasing negative skew as a parameter is increased
    • intermittency for the log-Poisson model
    • density-dependent timescales for Langevin (denser regions evolve faster)

1.4. TODO Effect of radiation pressure

  • The continuum radiation pressure and the line radiation pressure should both be considered
  • One thing that we can do is to calculate the radiative force at different points on the simulation and compare with the thermal pressure gradients
    • We can use the single-scattering limit and then correct it with the force multiplier for Lyman alpha
    • However, the H II gas itself is optically thin to the Lyman alpha (which is predominantly concentrated in the wings)
      • The main dynamical effect of the Lyman alpha will be on the expansion of the neutral gas
      • And this is most efficient when the neutral gas fully covers the ionizing source

1.7. Extension of the globule-plus-Stromgren-sphere model

  • We can add the stellar wind (or cluster wind) bubble into the mix
  • From the Lancaster papers (or others), we have the factor ξ, which is the ratio of the wind bubble diameter to the Stromgren radius
    • To find ξ they use a momentum-driven assumption but with a boost factor αp
    • From the numerical simulations in Lancaster 2025 they get αp in the range 3 to 8
    • This is due to an enhanced surface area of the contact discontinuity, modified by a slightly reduced velocity

1.10. Low filling factors in H II regions

  • Grazyna emailed to ask about this, but I am not sure that the empirical evidence is that convincing for very low ε.
  • She mentions 3 cases
    1. Hodge 1013 in M101
      • Stasinska:2013a
      • 6.5 Mpc away
      • This is a giant H II region, but it is not very well resolved spatially
      • Supposedly has ε = 0.003
        • rms density from surface brightness
        • compared with [S II] density to get filling factor
    2. Markarian 71
      • Luridiana:1999a
      • Note that this is not NGC 2363, despite that it is called that in the Valentina paper and others
      • Grazyna says filling factor of 0.003
      • But Valentina has several models with ε up to 0.1
        • Since [S II] density is upper limit
        • And she says that the best fits are for the higher filling factors
      • There is a lot of recent work on this object, since it is a local analog of Green Pea Galaxies and/or Lyman-continuum Emitters
        Micheva:2017a
        MRK 71 / NGC 2366: THE NEAREST GREEN PEA ANALOG
        Komorova:2021a
        Emission-line Wings Driven by Lyman Continuum in the Green Pea Analog Mrk 71
        • This one is particularly interesting. It proposes that the broad wings on Ha and [O III] are from a radiatively accelerated wind, and they claim that the high terminal velocity rules out dust, so it has to be Ly-c or Ly-alpha
        • However, they seem to think that the Ly-c can directly accelerate neutral gas, which is not right, since it is all absorbed in the H II region
        • Maybe Ly-alpha could do it, but the trouble with that is that the covering factor of the outflow has to be small, which would tend to burst the Ly-a bubble
        Smith:2023a
        HST FUV Spectroscopy of Super Star Cluster A in the Green Pea Analog Mrk 71: Revealing the Presence of Very Massive Stars
    3. NGC 346
      • Relano:2002a (summary of paper)
        • Photoionization models for N66
        • Mostly density bounded, 1-dimensional, spherical geometry
        • Models with ε = 1 have ionization that is too high and local density that is too low (compared with observations)
        • Best fit model is supposedly ε = 0.0127
        • Ionization-bounded models, and plane-parallel models do not work so well
      • My comments
        • I have written a paper on this region (https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MNRAS.529.1723H/abstract) and have two more in various states of disarray (here is draft of one: https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/0j4r9ys9d0oxewgt4gjkh/smc-bow.pdf?rlkey=tsdzi5ymaumqe2uqvba9oki0b&dl=0). This region is definitely not stellar-wind-dominated (there is a wind-driven bow shock around an O2 star, but this represents a small fraction of the total Ha emission). On the other hand, the geometry is of molecular filaments with a rather small covering factor around the star cluster, so a lot of the photo ionized gas is in divergent photoevaporation flows. This would also tend to give you a low filling factor if you tried to fit a spherical photoionization model to it.

1.11. High filling factors in H II regions

  • McClain:2025a study thousands of H II regions in NGC 253 with MUSE
    • Based on earlier paper Congiu:2025a
  • They find that the nebular size is universal, independent of the luminosity
    • Luminosity bins from log10 [L(Ha) / erg/s] = 35.8 (bin 1) to 38.05 (bin 10)
      • These are all extinction-corrected
      • Orion has log L(Ha) = 37.2, which would be bin 7
  • Criticism of their methods
    • In my opinion, they are over fitting the H II region structure, resulting in many small regions within what should be a single larger region
    • This is the reason that they get similar sizes for the different luminosity bins
  • Best fits to median profiles is double gaussian with deconvolved fwhm of 7 pc (30% of flux) and 30 pc (70% of flux)
  • They compare the s ii densities with the H alpha densities
    • For Ha the median central density is 25 pcc, with values
      • 51, 75, 124 for luminosity bins 8, 9, 10
      • down to 10-20 in bins 1, 2, 3
      • these are the true 3d peak
    • For s ii they get peak densities of
      • 26, 35, 69 for luminosity bins 8, 9, 10
      • but these are projected peak
    • They find that the s ii densities are smaller than H alpha densities
  • So this would give a filling factor larger than unity if taken at face value!
  • But we need to average the Ha density along the line of sight if we really want to compare them
    • [ ] Need to calculate some simple models to see how the various densities compare
    • But it doesn't look like the filling factors are going to come out to be small

1.12. PDF towards low densities and the DIG

  • In Orion, we have the EON, which has densities of order 100, so several sigma away from the mean density of the Huygens region
  • In general we have the DIG (Diffuse Ionized Gas), which supposedly represents about half of the integrated Ha emission from Galaxies
    • E.g., McClymont:2024a
    • In fact, the DIG is very different from the EON, and H II regions in general
    • Density less than 10 pcc
    • Ionizing spectrum is harder since it is more dominated by older stellar populations
      • Stars with ages up to 5 Myr mainly ionize H II regions
      • Stars with ages 5-10 Myr and 100+ Myr are the most imp
    • The half of the Ha emission statistic is only for observed L(Ha)
      • For the intrinsic L(Ha), it is only about 20%
      • The difference is due to the higher extinction of the H II regions, plus the scattering contribution to the DIG
